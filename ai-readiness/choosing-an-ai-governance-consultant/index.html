How to choose an AI governance consultant for a small business

A small business needs an AI governance consultant who can deliver an inventory of the AI tools your team already uses, a short acceptable-use policy, data rules, an approved-tool list, staff training and a review schedule, sized for 10 to 100 people. Ask for the deliverables, the timeline and who does the work before you sign.

What a small business should buy

You do not need an enterprise program or a committee. For a team of 10 to 100 people, a complete starting package looks like this:

  • An AI inventory: every AI tool your team uses, who uses it and what data it touches
  • An acceptable-use policy your team will actually read, a few pages long
  • Data rules for customer, financial and employee information
  • An approved-tool list, with business accounts and settings
  • A review step, so AI output is checked before it reaches a client
  • Staff training with your own examples
  • A review calendar, quarterly or twice a year
  • A link to a recognized standard, such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, so your controls hold up when a client, bank or auditor asks

If a proposal does not include most of these, ask why.

The four kinds of firms you will meet

Most firms offering AI governance to small businesses come from one of four backgrounds. Each can be the right fit; they just start in different places.

  • IT and managed service providers. Strong on tool settings, accounts and access. Check that they also cover policy, training and how work actually gets reviewed.
  • Cybersecurity firms. Strong on data protection and risk. Check that the policy is written for your staff, not only for your systems.
  • Operations-led firms. Start with how your business runs and where AI touches it. Check that they can speak to security controls too, directly or through partners.
  • Large advisory and accounting firms. Deep frameworks and audit experience. Check that the scope and price are sized for a small business.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What exactly will we have at the end: which documents, which settings, which training?
  • How long does it take, and what does it cost to start?
  • Who does the work, and what is their background in governance or audit?
  • Will you start by finding out which AI tools our team already uses?
  • How do you handle client, financial and employee data in the policy?
  • Which standard do you map to, and why?
  • How do you train staff, and with whose examples?
  • What happens after the first engagement: who owns the policy and how often is it reviewed?

A good consultant answers all of these in plain language, in the first call.

Red flags

  • A long policy nobody on your team will read
  • Tool recommendations before anyone has asked what your team already uses
  • No training, or training with generic examples
  • No named owner for the policy once the consultant leaves
  • A price or timeline they cannot give you even as a range

Frequently asked questions

Does a small business need an AI governance consultant?

Not always. If only one or two people use AI with no client or company data, a short written policy may be enough. Once several people use AI tools with client, financial or employee information, outside help usually saves time and closes gaps you would not spot yourself.

How much does AI governance consulting cost for a small business?

It varies by firm and scope. Ask every firm for a starting price or range and what it includes, and compare them on the same list of deliverables.

How long does it take?

For a small business, a focused starter engagement is usually measured in weeks, not months. Ask for the timeline in writing, with what you will have at the end of each stage.

Should we choose an IT firm or an operations firm?

Choose based on where your risk is. If your main gap is accounts and settings, an IT firm fits. If your main gap is how work gets done and checked, an operations-led firm fits. Many small businesses need a bit of both, so ask each firm how it covers the other half.
